1. What Is an Invoice?

An invoice is a document a business or self-employed professional sends to a customer to request payment for goods supplied or services completed. It records what was provided, how much is owed, and when payment should be made.

Clear invoices help customers understand charges and give both parties a useful record of the transaction. They can also make it easier to track outstanding payments and maintain business records.

An invoice is generally a request for payment. A receipt, by contrast, usually confirms that payment has already been received.

2. What Information Should an Invoice Include?

A professional invoice will usually include the following details:

  • Your business details: business name, address, and appropriate contact information.
  • Customer details: the customer's name or business name and relevant address.
  • Invoice number: a unique reference that helps you identify and track the invoice.
  • Invoice date: the date the invoice was issued.
  • Due date: when payment is expected.
  • Description: the products supplied or services provided.
  • Quantities and prices: the number of units or hours and their rates.
  • Subtotal: the amount before any applicable discount or tax.
  • Discounts and taxes: clearly identified amounts where applicable.
  • Total due: the final amount the customer needs to pay.
  • Payment instructions: accepted payment methods and any relevant reference.

Requirements vary by country, business type, and transaction. For example, registered businesses may need to include tax registration details and specific tax information. Check the rules that apply to your business and customer.

3. How to Create an Invoice Step by Step

Step 1: Enter your details

Add the correct business name and contact information. Use the details required by the regulations that apply to your business.

Step 2: Add your customer's details

Check the customer's name and billing information carefully. Incorrect details can cause confusion or delay payment.

Step 3: Describe the work or products

List each product or service separately where practical. Include quantities, hours, unit prices, or other details that explain the charge.

Step 4: Calculate the subtotal

Multiply each quantity by its unit price and add the line amounts together. Check the arithmetic before applying discounts or taxes.

Step 5: Apply discounts and applicable taxes

Show discounts and taxes clearly rather than hiding them inside an unexplained total. Tax rates and calculation rules depend on the relevant jurisdiction and transaction.

Step 6: Set payment terms

State the due date and explain how the customer can pay. Make sure the terms are agreed and consistent with any contract or applicable law.

Step 7: Review and send

Check the invoice number, customer details, dates, descriptions, calculations, and payment instructions. Save a copy for your records.

4. Worked Invoice Example

The following fictional example demonstrates how a subtotal, discount, and illustrative tax amount can be calculated. It is for explanation only, not tax advice.

Description Amount
Consulting service £100.00
Additional work £50.00
Subtotal £150.00
Discount (10% of £150) −£15.00
Amount after discount £135.00
Illustrative tax (20% of £135) £27.00
Total due £162.00

Calculation:

  • Subtotal: £100 + £50 = £150
  • Discount: £150 × 10% = £15
  • After discount: £150 − £15 = £135
  • Illustrative tax: £135 × 20% = £27
  • Total: £135 + £27 = £162

The tax rate and order of calculations used in a real invoice must follow the applicable tax rules. Do not charge tax unless you are entitled or required to do so under the rules that apply to you.

5. Payment Terms and Due Dates

Payment terms tell the customer when and how payment is expected. Common arrangements include payment immediately on receipt, within 7 days, within 14 days, or within 30 days.

These are examples, not universal rules. Agree the payment period with your customer and check any contractual or legal requirements.

To make payment easier:

  • Display the due date clearly.
  • Provide accurate payment instructions.
  • Include the invoice number as a payment reference if useful.
  • Keep a record of invoices sent and payments received.
  • Follow up politely on overdue invoices in line with your agreed terms.

6. Common Invoicing Mistakes to Avoid

Incorrect calculations

A small arithmetic error can lead to an incorrect amount being charged. Check quantities, unit prices, discounts, taxes, and the final total.

Missing invoice numbers

A consistent numbering system helps you locate documents and track payments. Avoid accidentally reusing an invoice number.

Unclear descriptions

A vague description can leave the customer unsure what they are paying for. Use clear descriptions that reflect the goods or services supplied.

Missing due dates

If the payment deadline is unclear, customers may not know when payment is expected. State the agreed due date.

Unclear tax treatment

Tax rules differ between countries and transactions. Check whether registration, tax collection, or specific invoice wording is required before issuing an invoice.

Not keeping copies

Keep copies of invoices and related records securely for the retention period required by the rules that apply to your business.

7. Invoice Checklist Before Sending

Use this checklist before sending an invoice to a customer:

  • Business and customer details are correct.
  • The invoice number is unique and easy to identify.
  • The issue date and due date are correct.
  • Descriptions, quantities, and unit prices are accurate.
  • Subtotal and discount calculations are correct.
  • Tax is handled according to applicable rules.
  • The total due is clear.
  • Payment instructions are complete.
  • A copy has been saved for your records.

Can I save an invoice as a PDF?

Yes. A browser's print function can often save a document as a PDF. Review the print preview to make sure the invoice fits the page and all important details are visible.

Does an invoice need a unique number?

A unique invoice reference is a useful way to identify, organise, and track invoices. Specific legal requirements depend on your location and business circumstances.

What is the difference between an invoice and a receipt?

An invoice generally requests payment. A receipt generally confirms that payment has been made. Keep the appropriate record for each transaction.

Do I need to charge tax on an invoice?

Not necessarily. Whether you must charge tax depends on your location, registration status, transaction, and applicable rules. Check official guidance or consult a qualified tax professional when needed.
